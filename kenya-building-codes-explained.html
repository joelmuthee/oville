Kenya Building Codes Explained: A Practical Guide for Developers

Eng. Oville Team April 2026 10 min read

The Building Code Landscape in Kenya

Kenya does not have a single, unified building code. Instead, compliance is governed by a stack of statutes, regulations, and County by-laws, each enforced by a different body. A compliant project must satisfy all of them simultaneously. Missing one is enough to halt occupation, void insurance, or attract a demolition order.

This guide walks through the main instruments, who enforces each, and where most developers slip up.

1. The Building Code (Local Government Adoptive By-Laws), 1968

Despite being legislated in 1968 and partially modernised since, this remains the foundational document. It covers:

  • Minimum room sizes and ceiling heights
  • Sanitary fittings per occupant
  • Ventilation and natural light requirements
  • Setbacks from property boundaries
  • Egress widths (staircase, corridor, door)
  • Fire-resisting construction in walls separating dwellings

It is dated, but each County has formally adopted it under its own by-laws, which keeps it operative. A successor document - the National Building Code 2024 - was published by the Ministry of Lands and is gradually being adopted but is not yet uniformly enforced.

2. The National Construction Authority Act, 2011

The NCA registers contractors in eight categories (NCA-1 down to NCA-8) by capacity and discipline. The Act requires:

  • Every construction project above a defined threshold to be registered with NCA before works begin
  • The contractor to be registered in a category equal to or above the project value
  • Mandatory site inspections at key stages
  • A levy of 0.5% of contract value, paid to NCA

NCA inspectors can issue stop-work notices, refuse occupation certificates, and prosecute non-compliant developers. They have done so on hundreds of Nairobi sites in the last five years.

3. The Engineers Act, 2011 (and EBK Regulations)

Covered in detail in our EBK article. Short version: structural, electrical, mechanical and public health engineering work must be designed and signed off by an EBK-registered Professional Engineer in the relevant field.

4. The Physical and Land Use Planning Act, 2019

Replaced the older Physical Planning Act. Governs zoning, change of use, sub-division, and the issuing of development permits at County level. Key compliance points:

  • Approved development permit from the County before any works begin
  • Compliance with the local Spatial Plan / Zoning Plan (plot ratio, ground coverage, permitted use class)
  • Change of user approval required when shifting between use classes (residential to commercial, for example)
  • Subdivision approval for splitting plots, with minimum size and access road requirements

5. NEMA and Environmental Impact Assessment

The Environmental Management and Co-ordination Act, 1999 (administered by NEMA) requires:

  • Project Report for smaller projects (typical for individual residential developments)
  • Full Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) for larger developments, industrial projects, or anything in environmentally sensitive zones (riparian, wetland, near protected areas)
  • EIA Licence issued before construction begins
  • Annual environmental audits for facilities with ongoing impacts

NEMA delays are one of the most common reasons projects in Kenya slip schedule. Start the EIA process at the same time as County planning approval, not afterwards.

6. The Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA), 2007

OSHA covers worker safety on construction sites and in completed workplaces. Compliance includes:

  • Registration of the workplace with the Directorate of Occupational Safety and Health Services (DOSHS)
  • Safety officer on site for projects above a threshold
  • PPE for all workers
  • Approved scaffolding, fall protection, lifting equipment
  • Periodic safety audits

7. Fire Safety: The Building Code + County Fire Brigade Approvals

Fire compliance is governed by the Building Code provisions plus County-level Fire Brigade approvals. Key requirements scale with building type and occupancy:

  • Compartmentation: fire-rated walls separating dwellings, escape routes, and high-risk rooms
  • Means of escape: minimum two stairs for buildings above 4 storeys, maximum travel distances to a protected stair
  • Fire fighting provision: hose reels, hydrants, sprinklers (mandatory in many commercial uses above defined floor areas)
  • Fire detection and alarm: smoke detection in residential corridors and exits, addressable systems in commercial buildings
  • Emergency lighting on escape routes
  • Fire brigade approval letter before occupation

8. KEBS and Material Standards

The Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) publishes Kenya Standards (KS) for construction materials: cement (KS EAS 18), steel reinforcement (KS 1748), concrete blocks, paint, electrical cables, sanitary fittings, and more. Materials used on a project should carry the KEBS Standardisation Mark or equivalent recognised standard.

The most commonly faked or substituted items in Kenya are: cement (counterfeit packaging), rebar (under-rolled or non-compliant Chinese imports), and electrical cables (under-rated copper). Specifying KEBS-approved suppliers in the BOQ is the first defence; spot-testing on delivery is the second.

9. County-Specific By-Laws

Each County has its own development control by-laws layered on top of the national framework. Nairobi County's by-laws are the most elaborate. Common variations:

  • Plot ratios and ground coverage by zone (Nairobi has dozens of zones, each with its own limits)
  • Required parking provision per use type
  • Setback distances
  • Boundary wall height limits
  • Riparian reserve widths for plots adjacent to rivers
  • Tree preservation requirements

Where Most Developers Slip

1. Starting construction before NEMA approval

Common in residential developments. NEMA can issue a stop-work order and the developer must pay penalties before construction can resume.

2. Engaging a contractor in the wrong NCA category

An NCA-5 contractor on a job that requires NCA-3 capacity. The NCA can decline to register the project and you must re-tender.

3. Skipping the EBK stamp on structural drawings

The County plans officer may pass the submission anyway, but the building is then non-compliant from day one. Insurance issues follow.

4. Building outside the approved envelope

Adding floors, balconies, or footprint not on the approved drawings. The County may refuse to issue the Certificate of Occupation or order partial demolition.

5. Treating Fire Brigade approval as a final-stage tick-box

Fire compliance must be designed in from the start (stair geometry, compartmentation, fire mains). Retrofitting fire compliance into a near-complete building is expensive and sometimes impossible.

The Compliance Checklist

Before construction starts on any Kenyan project, the developer should hold:

  • Approved County development permit
  • NEMA EIA Licence (or Project Report acceptance)
  • Approved structural, architectural, electrical, mechanical, and public health drawings, all stamped by EBK-registered engineers
  • NCA project registration certificate
  • Contractor in the right NCA category
  • DOSHS workplace registration
  • Fire Brigade design-stage approval
  • Any specialist approvals (KCAA for airport zones, KFS for forest-adjacent plots, etc.)

The cost of getting compliance right is a small fraction of the cost of getting it wrong. We routinely see developers pay 5-15% of project value to remedy compliance gaps discovered late, on top of holding costs while the building sits empty.
