Concrete Testing in Nairobi: Methods, Cost, and What to Expect

Eng. Oville Team April 2026 9 min read

Why Concrete Testing Matters in Nairobi

Nairobi consumes more ready-mix and site-batched concrete than any other city in East Africa. The quality of that concrete varies enormously, from C40 ready-mix from established suppliers to site-batched mixes where the cement-to-sand ratio depends on whichever wheelbarrow the foreman picked up first. Concrete testing is how you find out which one you actually have in your building.

Testing happens at three points in a building's life:

  1. Before the pour - to confirm the fresh mix is workable and will achieve the design strength
  2. After the pour - to confirm the hardened concrete reached the design strength at 7, 14, and 28 days
  3. Years later - to confirm the existing concrete is still sound, or to investigate a problem

Fresh Concrete Tests

1. Slump Test

The slump test measures how stiff or workable a fresh concrete mix is. A slump cone (300 mm tall, 200 mm base, 100 mm top) is filled, lifted, and the concrete's drop is measured.

Typical slump targets in Nairobi: 50-100 mm for slabs, 75-125 mm for columns, 150-180 mm for pumped concrete in high-rise work.

Cost: Bundled with cube sampling, typically KES 1,500-3,000 per test.

What it tells you: Whether the mix is too dry to compact properly or too wet (which means water has been added on site, which reduces strength).

2. Cube Sampling

Six concrete cubes (150 mm) are cast from each significant pour and cured under controlled conditions. Three are crushed at 7 days, three at 28 days. The 28-day crushing strength is the formal compliance test against the design grade.

Cost in Nairobi: KES 2,500-4,500 per set of 6 cubes including pickup, curing, and crushing at a NABL or KEBS-recognised lab.

How many sets: One set per 50 cubic metres or per pour, whichever is more, as per BS 8500.

Where this fails: Many Nairobi contractors cast cubes but never collect or test them. The cubes sit in a corner of the site, the contractor signs off on the building, and there is no compliance evidence at the end.

Hardened Concrete Tests on Existing Buildings

3. Schmidt Hammer (Rebound) Test

A spring-loaded plunger is fired against the concrete surface. The hammer measures how much the plunger rebounds. Harder concrete bounces it back further. The reading converts to an estimated compressive strength.

Cost in Nairobi: KES 2,000-5,000 per test location, with a typical audit covering 30-80 locations.

Best for: Quickly mapping concrete strength across a large area (a slab, a wall, a column line). Excellent for spotting weak zones that need deeper investigation.

Limitations: Reading the surface only (top 20-30 mm), affected by surface carbonation and moisture. Indicative, not definitive.

4. Core Sampling

A diamond-tipped core drill cuts a 75 mm or 100 mm diameter cylinder out of the structure. The cylinder is sent to a lab and crushed in a calibrated press.

Cost in Nairobi: KES 8,000-15,000 per core including drilling, lab testing, and a make-good plug.

Best for: Definitive confirmation of in-situ strength when Schmidt hammer results are suspicious or when a dispute is in play.

Limitations: Destructive. Each core leaves a hole that must be made good. Typically 6-10 cores per audit, located carefully to avoid weakening critical elements.

5. Carbonation Depth Testing

A phenolphthalein indicator is sprayed on a freshly broken concrete face. Sound (high-pH) concrete turns pink. Carbonated (low-pH) concrete stays grey. The depth of the grey zone is the carbonation depth.

Cost in Nairobi: KES 1,500-3,000 per location, often bundled with core sampling.

Why it matters: Carbonation reaches the rebar means corrosion starts. In Nairobi's climate, a building with 25 mm cover and 0.5 mm/year carbonation has roughly 50 years before corrosion begins. If carbonation is faster or cover is thinner, the timeline shortens.

6. Ferroscan / GPR Rebar Survey

Not strictly a concrete test, but always done alongside. Maps where the steel is, how deep it sits, and how thick the cover is. Covered in detail in our Ferroscan testing article.

Concrete Quality Issues Common in Nairobi

1. Under-strength site-batched concrete

Site batching by the head pan ("debe") is common on smaller jobs. Without weigh-batching, cement-to-aggregate ratios drift. Schmidt hammer surveys on these buildings routinely show actual strengths 20-40% below design. This is one of the most consistent findings we see.

2. Excessive water addition on site

Ready-mix arrives on a hot Nairobi afternoon and the foreman adds a hose to make it more workable. Every extra litre of water reduces strength. By 28 days, a C30 mix that received 20 litres extra per cubic metre might test at C22 or lower.

3. Curing failures

Nairobi's dry season is brutal on fresh concrete. Without continuous wetting or curing compound for at least 7 days, surface concrete loses 15-25% of its potential strength. We see this most often on flat slabs poured at the start of the long dry spell in July-September.

4. Carbonation in older buildings

Buildings from the 1970s and 1980s in Industrial Area, South B, and Westlands often show carbonation depths of 20-35 mm. Where cover is 25 mm or less, rebar is already corroding and spalling is visible at slab soffits.

5. Aggregate quality variation

Coarse aggregate quality varies between quarries. Some sources have flaky particles, dust contamination, or alkali-reactive minerals that cause long-term cracking (alkali-silica reaction).

Choosing a Concrete Testing Provider

Three things matter when picking who tests your concrete:

  1. EBK registration for the supervising engineer. Without it, the report has no professional weight.
  2. Calibrated equipment. Schmidt hammers drift with use. Ask when the hammer was last calibrated and against what reference.
  3. Lab accreditation. Cube and core crushing should be done at a KEBS-recognised or NABL-accredited lab, not someone's back yard.

If a quotation is far cheaper than the ranges above, ask what is being skipped. The most common shortcuts are: no calibration trace on the hammer, no lab accreditation, no rebar survey before drilling cores (and a damaged column afterwards).
