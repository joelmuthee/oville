Structural Assessment vs Structural Audit: What's the Difference?

Eng. Oville Team April 2026 8 min read

The Short Answer

A structural assessment is a high-level evaluation of whether a building looks structurally sound. It is often visual, fast, and inexpensive. A structural audit is a deep, evidence-based investigation that combines visual inspection with instrumented testing, calculations, and a formal verdict on safety and remaining service life.

People in Kenya use the two terms interchangeably. The County, NCA inspectors, insurers, banks and tenants do not. Asking for the wrong one wastes money or produces a report that does not answer the question you actually had.

Structural Assessment: Scope and Purpose

A structural assessment is essentially a structured walkthrough by a qualified engineer. The engineer looks at the building, takes notes, photographs visible defects, and produces a short report with a professional opinion. No instruments. No core samples. No calculations beyond rule-of-thumb sanity checks.

What it includes

  • Site walkthrough of the entire building (typically 2-4 hours for a mid-rise)
  • Visual inspection of slabs, beams, columns, walls, foundations where accessible
  • Photographic record of cracks, water damage, deflections, corrosion staining
  • Brief interview with caretaker or owner about known issues
  • Engineer's qualitative judgement: "appears sound", "needs further investigation", "do not occupy"

What it does NOT include

  • Concrete strength testing (no Schmidt hammer, no cores)
  • Rebar location or cover measurement (no Ferroscan or GPR)
  • Re-analysis of the structure under current loads or codes
  • Review of original drawings or design calculations
  • Geotechnical or soil testing

Typical cost and time in Kenya

For a standalone house or small block of flats, a structural assessment in Nairobi costs KES 35,000 to 80,000 and the report is usually delivered within a week. For a mid-rise commercial building, expect KES 80,000 to 200,000.

Structural Audit: Scope and Purpose

A structural audit is what you commission when an opinion is not enough. The engineer must produce evidence that will stand up in front of NCA, an insurer, a bank, a court, or a buyer's due diligence team. That requires measurements, not impressions.

What it includes

  • Everything in an assessment, plus the items below
  • Non-destructive testing: Schmidt hammer for concrete strength, Ferroscan or GPR for rebar location and cover
  • Selective destructive testing where justified: core samples, rebar exposure, carbonation depth
  • Recovery and review of original structural drawings and design calculations where available
  • Re-analysis of critical elements (columns, transfer beams, foundations) using current loads and current code
  • Geotechnical input where settlement, cracking, or expansive soils are suspected
  • Formal verdict on each structural element: compliant, marginal, deficient
  • Remedial recommendations with quantities and an order-of-magnitude budget
  • Statement of remaining service life subject to the recommended remedials

Typical cost and time in Kenya

A full audit of a mid-rise commercial building in Nairobi typically costs KES 300,000 to 1,200,000 and takes 3 to 8 weeks from site mobilisation to final report. High-rises, mixed-use complexes, and forensic audits cost more.

When an Assessment Is Enough

A structural assessment is the right tool when you need a quick, qualified opinion before deciding what to do next. Common triggers:

  • Pre-purchase walkthrough on a small residential property where the budget for the report is limited
  • After a minor incident (small fire, water leak, minor vehicle impact) to decide whether deeper investigation is needed
  • Cracks have appeared in plaster and the owner wants to know if they should worry before commissioning a full investigation
  • Insurance broker asks for a sign-off letter on a low-risk asset
  • Pre-renovation sanity check before scoping out detailed design work

When You Must Have a Full Audit

If a regulator, court, insurer, bank, or buyer is going to make a decision based on your report, you need an audit, not an assessment. An opinion does not survive cross-examination.

  • NCA Form B2 or a county safety notice has been issued against the building
  • Change of use (residential to commercial, office to clinic, warehouse to assembly)
  • Adding floors or increasing imposed loads on an existing building
  • Visible structural distress: diagonal cracks in columns, severe slab deflection, rebar exposure, spalling
  • Building age over 30 years with no documented previous audit
  • Investor due diligence on a portfolio acquisition
  • Post-incident: fire, flood, vehicle or crane impact, partial collapse, neighbouring excavation
  • Forensic dispute: latent defect claim, professional indemnity matter, fatality investigation

The Two-Step Approach

The most economical path for most Kenyan clients is to start with an assessment and escalate to an audit only if the assessment flags something. This avoids paying audit-level fees on buildings that turn out to be straightforward.

A good assessment will tell you one of three things:

  1. No further work needed. The building is in expected condition for its age and use.
  2. Targeted investigation needed. Specific elements (one column line, the foundation, a transfer beam) warrant testing. This is cheaper than a full audit.
  3. Full audit required. Enough warning signs are present that opinion-level evidence is no longer adequate.

What Goes Wrong When the Wrong One Is Commissioned

We are regularly asked to "second-opinion" reports that did not match the client's actual need:

  • Buyer commissioned an assessment to satisfy a bank that wanted an audit. The bank rejected it and the deal stalled for two months.
  • Owner commissioned a full audit on a six-month-old building when an assessment plus a snag list would have done the job for one-tenth the price.
  • Insurer was given an assessment after a small fire. They argued for nine months that the structure was unsafe; the matter was eventually resolved only after a proper audit confirmed it was sound.

Picking the Right Engineer

Both assessments and audits must be carried out by a professional engineer registered with the Engineers Board of Kenya (EBK). For audits, the firm should also be registered with NCA and able to demonstrate access to NDT equipment (Schmidt hammer, Ferroscan, or GPR) and a track record of similar work.

Ask for sample reports. A real audit report runs to 40-100 pages with photographs, scan plots, calculation summaries, and a defensible verdict. A real assessment report runs to 8-20 pages with photographs and a clear opinion. Anything shorter or thinner than that is unlikely to do the job.
