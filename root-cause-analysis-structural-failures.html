Root Cause Analysis of Structural Failures: How Forensic Engineers Work

Eng. Oville Team April 2026 10 min read

What Root Cause Analysis Means in Structural Engineering

Root cause analysis (RCA) is the disciplined process of working backwards from a structural failure to identify the underlying causes, not just the visible trigger. The collapse, the crack, the deflection - those are symptoms. The root cause is the chain of decisions, materials, and conditions that made the failure possible.

Done well, RCA produces a defensible technical narrative that explains:

  • What failed first (the initiating element or event)
  • How the failure propagated through the structure
  • Why that element was vulnerable in the first place
  • What decisions, designs, materials or actions created the vulnerability
  • What could have been done at any of those points to prevent the outcome

The audience for an RCA is usually a court, an insurer, a regulator, or all three. The standard of evidence required is much higher than for a routine audit.

The Five Phases of a Structural RCA

Phase 1: Site Securing and Evidence Preservation

The investigation begins the moment the failure is reported. Critical actions in the first 24-72 hours:

  • Co-ordinate with County, NCA, police and emergency services to establish access
  • Identify and preserve the failure debris field - locations of fallen elements must be recorded before any clearing
  • Photograph the scene comprehensively from multiple angles before anything is moved
  • Locate and secure original construction documents (drawings, calculations, materials certificates, cube test records)
  • Identify witnesses: workers on site, occupants, neighbours, contractors. Get statements while memory is fresh
  • Take material samples - rebar fragments, concrete chunks, soil at foundations - before they are lost

Evidence lost in this phase cannot be recovered later. We have seen forensic cases collapse because the site was cleared within 48 hours of the incident on the instruction of the developer's insurer, before the structural investigator arrived.

Phase 2: Data Gathering

Once the scene is secured, systematic data collection:

  • Detailed survey of the failure (3D laser scan where the geometry is complex)
  • Mapping of crack patterns, deflections, and displacements on remaining elements
  • Material testing - concrete cores, rebar samples, soil samples - sent to accredited labs
  • Non-destructive testing of standing elements (Schmidt hammer, Ferroscan)
  • Document review: original design, construction records, approval correspondence, maintenance logs, weather data, recent works on site
  • Interviews with all relevant personnel

Phase 3: Reconstruction

From the data, the investigator builds a model of the structure as it was at the moment of failure. This includes:

  • The intended design (as drawn and calculated)
  • The as-built condition (where it differed from the design)
  • The deteriorated condition at the time of failure (corrosion, carbonation, prior cracking)
  • The loading at the moment of failure (live loads, environmental loads, any unusual event)

Computational analysis often follows: finite-element models of the structure under the reconstructed loading, looking for the element that fails first when the analysis is run with realistic material properties.

Phase 4: Hypothesis Testing

Investigators rarely start with a single theory. They start with several plausible failure mechanisms and test each against the evidence:

  1. Overloading - was the load at failure greater than design?
  2. Material deficiency - was concrete strength or rebar specification below design?
  3. Design error - did the design meet code for the imposed loads?
  4. Construction defect - was the structure built as designed?
  5. Foundation failure - did the ground move or settle differentially?
  6. External event - was there impact, fire, or excavation nearby?
  7. Maintenance neglect - did long-term deterioration reduce capacity below the actual load?

Each hypothesis must be consistent with all the evidence. If a hypothesis explains the column failure but cannot explain why the cracks ran in a particular pattern, it is not the right one. The right hypothesis explains every observation.

Phase 5: Reporting and Defence

The final report sets out:

  • The factual record (what was observed, measured, tested)
  • The methodology (how the analysis was carried out)
  • The hypotheses considered and the basis on which each was accepted or rejected
  • The conclusions on root cause, immediate cause, and contributory factors
  • Recommendations - both for the specific case and for prevention of similar events

The report must be defensible under cross-examination. Every conclusion must trace back to specific evidence. Every measurement must be referenced. Every assumption must be stated and justified.

Common Root Causes in Kenyan Building Failures

Across the failures Oville has investigated in Kenya, the underlying root causes cluster around a small number of themes:

1. Inadequate Foundations on Expansive or Compressible Soils

Black cotton, alluvial deposits and fill receive shallow foundations they cannot support. The collapse is triggered by a wet season or a nearby excavation, but the root cause is the foundation design.

2. Under-Strength Concrete in Critical Elements

Site-batched concrete reaches 60-70% of specified strength in columns and beams. Buildings stand for years until an additional load (another floor, change of use, accumulated dead load from new finishes) tips a critical element past its actual capacity.

3. Rebar Substitution or Omission

Specified rebar grade or quantity is not what was placed. Bars are missing from corners, links are spaced wider than design, or lower-grade imported rebar is used. Failure mode is brittle, with little warning.

4. Illegal Modifications

Floors added, columns removed, beams cut for service penetrations - all without engineering review. The structure no longer matches its design assumptions. Sometimes a partial collapse follows immediately; sometimes the structure stands until a marginal event tips it over.

5. Construction Sequence Errors

Formwork stripped too early. Slabs loaded with stacked blocks before achieving strength. Backfill placed against basement walls before the slab above is complete. Each error reduces margin, and combinations of errors eliminate it.

6. Maintenance Neglect

Roof leaks left unaddressed for years. Water tracks down through reinforced concrete, corrodes rebar from inside, and the building fails when corrosion section loss exceeds capacity. Common in older buildings in industrial zones.

RCA Tools and Frameworks

The 5 Whys

Repeatedly asking "why" drills past symptoms to root causes:

  • Why did the column fail? - Concrete was below design strength
  • Why was concrete below strength? - Site batching gave variable mix
  • Why did site batching produce variable mix? - No weigh-batching, mix by head pan
  • Why was head-pan batching used? - No supervision insisted on calibrated batching
  • Why no insistence on calibrated batching? - No EBK-registered engineer on site during construction

The root cause is the supervision gap, not the column itself.

Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

A top-down logic diagram. The top event ("column failed") is decomposed into all possible causes, each of which is decomposed further, until each branch reaches a basic event that either happened or did not.

Ishikawa (Fishbone) Diagram

The failure is the spine; the contributing categories (material, method, machine, manpower, environment, management) are the bones. Used to map all factors before testing each.

Common Mistakes That Compromise an Investigation

1. Clearing debris too soon

Insurer or developer pressure to "make the site safe" before the investigator arrives. Critical evidence is lost.

2. Single-hypothesis investigations

The investigator decides early what caused the failure and looks only for confirming evidence. Disconfirming evidence is dismissed or never sought. The eventual report cannot survive scrutiny.

3. Using only one method

Visual inspection alone, without NDT. Or analytical modelling without site measurement. The strongest RCAs triangulate from multiple independent sources.

4. Not interviewing the right people

The site foreman, the rebar fixer, the concrete supplier driver - these people know things the design team and the developer do not. Investigators who only talk to the principals miss key facts.

5. Mixing root cause with blame

RCA identifies causes, not culpability. Recommending sanctions in the report compromises its independence. Recommendations should be technical: "specify weigh-batching for concrete" not "the contractor was negligent."

The purpose of an RCA is to prevent recurrence, not to allocate blame. Even when blame is the eventual outcome (in court or in arbitration), the technical RCA should be neutral so its findings hold up regardless of who is on which side.
